Classical Tests: z, t, Ranks, and Distributions

Learning goals

You should know when to use z, Student, paired, pooled, and Welch tests; explain the roles of chi-square, Mann–Whitney, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, and Kruskal–Wallis tests; and check each test’s design assumptions. Review sampling distributions and null hypotheses first.

Intuition

A significance test compares an observed discrepancy with discrepancies expected under a null model. A z test treats the standard error as known. A t test accounts for estimating spread from the sample, producing heavier reference tails at low degrees of freedom. Rank and empirical-distribution tests answer different questions when a mean comparison is not the target.

Worked example

Suppose independent groups have observations \(A=(1,2,3)\) and \(B=(2,3,4)\). Their means differ by \(-1\); each sample variance is \(1\). Welch’s statistic is

\[ t=\frac{\bar x_A-\bar x_B}{\sqrt{s_A^2/n_A+s_B^2/n_B}} =\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1/3+1/3}}\approx-1.225. \]

The Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom are \(4\) in this equal-size, equal-variance example. A two-sided \(t_4\) reference distribution gives a large p-value; the sample is too small to establish a mean difference reliably. The interpretation concerns means under independent sampling, not every aspect of the distributions.

For paired data, use within-pair differences before testing. If four before-minus-after differences are \((1,2,3,4)\), their mean is \(2.5\), their sample SD is \(\sqrt{5/3}\), and \(t=2.5/(\sqrt{5/3}/2)\approx3.873\) with \(3\) degrees of freedom. Treating these as eight independent measurements would discard the pairing.

Theory and assumptions

Tests of means

With known population SD, the one-sample statistic is \(z=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\). With unknown SD, replace \(\sigma\) by \(s\) to obtain \(t=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)\), with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom for an exact normal-sample t test. Normality of the raw observations or pair differences matters for exact small-sample t calibration; at larger sizes, mean-based inference may be robust under suitable finite-variance conditions. Strong dependence or severe skew can still cause problems.

For two independent groups, the pooled test assumes equal population variances:

\[ s_p^2=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2},\qquad t=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1+1/n_2}},\quad df=n_1+n_2-2. \]

The paired test applies the one-sample t formula to differences \(d_i\), testing \(E[D]=0\) with \(df=n-1\). The Welch test avoids an equal-variance assumption:

\[ t=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}},\qquad \nu\approx\frac{(s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2)^2}{(s_1^2/n_1)^2/(n_1-1)+(s_2^2/n_2)^2/(n_2-1)}. \]

Welch is often a sensible default for independent mean comparisons. Equal variance is required by the pooled test, not by all parametric methods.

Counts, ranks, and distribution functions

Question Statistic and reference Key condition
Do categorical counts fit prespecified probabilities? Pearson \(\chi^2=\sum (O-E)^2/E\), approximately \(\chi^2_{k-1}\) for \(k\) fixed categories Independent counts and sufficiently informative expected cells; estimated parameters reduce degrees of freedom.
Are two categorical variables associated? Pearson chi-square on an \(r\times c\) table, approximately \(\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}\) Independent units and adequate expected counts; small sparse tables may need an exact method.
Do two independent ordered samples differ in stochastic position? Mann–Whitney \(U\), a rank-sum statistic Independent observations; a pure median-shift interpretation needs similar distribution shapes.
Are two distribution functions the same? Two-sample KS \(D=\sup_x|F_n(x)-G_m(x)|\) Independent samples; usual distribution-free calibration assumes a continuous null.
Do three or more independent groups differ in ranks? Kruskal–Wallis \(H\), approximately \(\chi^2_{g-1}\) Independent groups; tied ranks and small samples need appropriate correction/calibration.

For Mann–Whitney, if \(R_1\) is the rank sum of group 1, one common orientation is \(U_1=R_1-n_1(n_1+1)/2\) and \(U_2=n_1n_2-U_1\). Some texts reverse which group is counted; two-sided testing is unaffected. Without ties under its null, \(E[U_1]=n_1n_2/2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(U_1)=n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)/12\). Exact enumeration is useful at small sizes; normal approximation improves with adequate data and a tie correction.

For a one-sample KS test, compare the empirical CDF to a fully specified CDF. Estimating that CDF’s parameters from the same data changes the null distribution; standard KS critical values then do not apply directly. Kruskal–Wallis uses total rank sum \(R_j\) within each group of size \(n_j\):

\[ H=\frac{12}{N(N+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{R_j^2}{n_j}-3(N+1),\qquad N=\sum_jn_j. \]

Practice and pitfalls

  • Ask which outcome and estimand matter: a difference in means, ranks, category probabilities, or entire distributions. A “nonparametric replacement for t” does not necessarily test the same null.
  • Pairing, clustering, repeated measurement, and missing data must shape the test. No distributional test repairs confounded treatment assignment.
  • A Mann–Whitney result is not automatically a median difference; unequal spread alone can generate a rank difference.
  • Chi-square and KS reference distributions may be poor with sparse cells, ties, or estimated parameters. Use exact or design-aware alternatives when needed.

Interview questions

When would you choose Welch over pooled t? For two independent means when equal variance is not justified, particularly with different group sizes. Welch estimates each variance separately and adjusts degrees of freedom.

What does the Mann–Whitney test compare? It uses pairwise ordering or ranks. Under suitable conditions it tests equality of distributions; interpreting it specifically as a location or median shift requires additional shape assumptions.

Why are z and t different? Estimating an unknown SD adds uncertainty. The t reference has heavier tails, especially with few degrees of freedom.

Summary

Choose a test from the question, outcome scale, grouping structure, and null model. Formula selection is the last step after checking independence, pairing, variance, and calibration.
