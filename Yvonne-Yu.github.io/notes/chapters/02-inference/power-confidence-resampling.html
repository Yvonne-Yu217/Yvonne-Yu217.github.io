Power, Confidence Intervals, Bootstrap, and Jackknife

Learning goals

You should distinguish type I and II errors, calculate a simple standard-error-based interval, interpret frequentist coverage, relate power to sample size and effect magnitude, and choose between bootstrap and jackknife resampling.

Intuition

Power is the probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when a specified alternative is true. A low-powered study often produces a wide interval, making both a nonzero effect and a practically negligible effect compatible with the data. Confidence intervals foreground a range of effects under a procedure; resampling estimates the procedure’s variability when an analytic formula is inconvenient.

Worked example

Suppose a sample mean is \(100\), population SD is known to be \(20\), and \(n=100\) independent observations satisfy conditions for a normal approximation. The standard error is \(20/\sqrt{100}=2\), so a 95% z interval is

\[ 100\pm1.96(2)=[96.08,103.92]. \]

For a point-null difference of zero, an interval narrow enough to lie inside a prespecified negligible-effect region—say \([-5,5]\) in effect units—could support a practical equivalence claim via the appropriate equivalence procedure. A wide interval crossing zero gives less information; “not significant” alone cannot distinguish these situations.

For resampling, take observations \((1,2,3)\) with mean \(2\). One size-three bootstrap draw with replacement might be \((1,1,3)\), whose mean is \(5/3\); many such draws form an empirical distribution of means. The three leave-one-out jackknife means are \(2.5,2,1.5\). Their mean is \(2\), giving jackknife variance \((3-1)/3[(0.5)^2+0^2+(-0.5)^2]=1/3\) and standard error \(\sqrt{1/3}\approx0.577\).

Theory and assumptions

Type I, type II, and power

Decision \(H_0\) true Specified alternative true
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error, rate \(\alpha\) Correct detection, power \(1-\beta\)
Do not reject \(H_0\) Correct non-rejection, rate \(1-\alpha\) Type II error, rate \(\beta\)

Power depends on the alternative effect, variance, sample size, threshold, sidedness, and design. Lowering \(\alpha\) while holding other factors fixed usually reduces power. Increasing sample size narrows the standard error. Pairing can improve precision when within-pair outcomes are positively correlated, but poor pairing or attrition can erase the gain.

For an approximate two-sided one-sample z test of a known-SD mean difference \(\delta\), target power \(1-\beta\) gives

\[ n\approx\left[\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})\sigma}{\delta}\right]^2. \]

At \(\alpha=0.05\), 80% target power, \(\sigma=20\), and \(\delta=5\), this is about \([(1.96+0.84)4]^2=125.44\), so round up to at least \(126\) one-sample observations before allowances for attrition or approximation. An equal-size two-arm comparison has a different variance and needs a larger per-arm number. The source’s shorthand “power \(\propto\) effect size \(\times\sqrt N\)” captures the signal-to-noise trend but is not a general power equation.

Confidence intervals and evidence of absence

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence procedure covers a fixed parameter in \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of repetitions under its assumptions. Once an interval is observed, the frequentist parameter is not assigned a probability of being inside it. For an iid normal mean with unknown SD, use \(\bar x\pm t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}s/\sqrt n\); a known-SD z interval uses \(\sigma\). Intervals from clustered data, ratios, bounded parameters, or skewed estimators need appropriate calibration.

Absence of evidence means the interval still includes effects large enough to matter. Evidence of practical absence requires an interval sufficiently precise to exclude effects outside a prespecified equivalence region, with a method suited to the claim. Simply crossing zero does not decide this issue.

Bootstrap and jackknife

The nonparametric bootstrap treats the observed empirical distribution as an approximation to the population. Repeatedly sample \(n\) observations with replacement, compute the statistic \(\hat\theta_b^*\), and use the distribution across draws to estimate standard error or an interval. A percentile interval takes suitable quantiles of \(\hat\theta_b^*\); skew, bias, boundaries, dependence, and small samples can make simple percentile intervals inaccurate. Alternatives include studentized or bias-corrected approaches when justified. Clustered or time-dependent observations need a resampling scheme that preserves dependence.

The jackknife deterministically removes one observation at a time. For leave-one-out estimates \(\hat\theta_{(i)}\) and their mean \(\bar\theta_{(.)}\),

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\rm jack}(\hat\theta)=\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\hat\theta_{(i)}-\bar\theta_{(.)})^2. \]

It also yields a bias estimate, \(\widehat{\operatorname{bias}}_{\rm jack}=(n-1)(\bar\theta_{(.)}-\hat\theta)\). Jackknife and bootstrap are distinct methods, not simply deterministic and stochastic versions of one another. Jackknife can perform poorly for nonsmooth statistics such as some quantiles; bootstrap is not automatically valid either.

Practice and pitfalls

  • Define the minimum effect of interest and target power before data collection; a post hoc power number rarely adds more than the observed estimate and interval.
  • The sample cannot resample a truly absent rare event. Bigger samples reduce sampling variability but do not cure selection bias.
  • A bootstrap distribution approximates uncertainty conditional on the observed sampling mechanism. It does not replace randomization or a defensible target population.
  • Report the interval construction, resampling unit, repetitions, and treatment of ties or failures for reproducibility.

Interview questions

What does 80% power mean? If a particular alternative effect and all design assumptions hold, the prespecified test rejects the null in about 80% of repeated studies.

Does a 95% confidence interval put 95% probability on this parameter being inside? Not in the frequentist interpretation. The procedure has 95% long-run coverage under its assumptions.

Why use bootstrap instead of a textbook formula? A complicated statistic may not have a convenient analytic standard error. I resample the correct unit and validate that the empirical distribution approximates the estimator’s sampling behavior.

Summary

Power addresses whether a planned study can detect a meaningful effect. Intervals show the precision of an estimate. Bootstrap and jackknife estimate uncertainty from observed data, subject to the original data’s quality and the statistic’s regularity.
