Bayesian Updating, Bayes Factors, and Predictive Value

Learning goals

You should calculate a posterior with Bayes’ rule, distinguish likelihood from probability and maximum likelihood from Bayesian estimation, interpret a credible interval, explain a Bayes factor, and derive the positive predictive value of a significant finding.

Intuition

Bayes’ rule updates prior uncertainty when data arrive. A frequentist parameter is conventionally a fixed unknown quantity and probability refers to a repeatable procedure or random data. A Bayesian model assigns a probability distribution to the unknown parameter conditional on the model and prior. Neither interpretation turns poor data into strong evidence.

For an event \(A\) and observation \(B\) with \(P(B)>0\),

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)} =\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid\neg A)P(\neg A)}. \]

The numerator combines a prior \(P(A)\) and a likelihood of the evidence \(P(B\mid A)\); the denominator is the marginal probability of the evidence, which normalizes the posterior.

Worked example

Updating a conversion probability

Suppose \(7\) of \(10\) independent trials succeed. Under a Bernoulli model, the likelihood as a function of the unknown success probability \(p\) is proportional to \(p^7(1-p)^3\); its maximum is the MLE \(\hat p=7/10=0.7\). With a \(\operatorname{Beta}(2,2)\) prior, conjugate updating gives \(p\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(9,5)\), whose posterior mean is \(9/14\approx0.643\). A 95% posterior credible interval contains 95% of this model-conditional posterior mass; its endpoints are posterior quantiles, not \(\hat p\pm1.96\) standard errors.

The prior matters at this sample size. A uniform \(\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\) prior gives \(\operatorname{Beta}(8,4)\) and mean \(2/3\), while a concentrated \(\operatorname{Beta}(20,20)\) prior gives \(\operatorname{Beta}(27,23)\) and mean \(0.54\). Report such sensitivity when prior information is debatable.

Bayes factor for two point hypotheses

Compare \(H_0:p=0.5\) with \(H_1:p=0.7\) for the same \(7\) successes and \(3\) failures. The binomial coefficient cancels in the evidence ratio:

\[ BF_{10}=\frac{P(D\mid H_1)}{P(D\mid H_0)} =\frac{0.7^7 0.3^3}{0.5^{10}}\approx2.28. \]

With equal prior odds, posterior odds for \(H_1\) are \(2.28:1\) and \(P(H_1\mid D,H_0\text{ or }H_1)\approx2.28/3.28=0.695\). This posterior probability is conditional on treating the two point hypotheses as exhaustive and assigning equal prior probabilities.

Theory and assumptions

Likelihood and MLE

For fixed \(\theta\), \(p(x\mid\theta)\) describes a sampling model for possible data \(x\). After observing \(x\), the same mathematical expression viewed as a function of \(\theta\) is the likelihood \(L(\theta;x)\). A likelihood is not generally a normalized probability distribution over \(\theta\). For iid data, \(L(\theta;x_{1:n})=\prod_i p(x_i\mid\theta)\); independence must be justified, not assumed by notation. Maximum likelihood selects the \(\theta\) that maximizes this function, often by maximizing its log.

Bayesian updating uses a prior density \(\pi(\theta)\) and posterior \(\pi(\theta\mid D)\propto p(D\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)\). The normalizing constant is \(p(D)=\int p(D\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta\). A 95% credible interval \(C(D)\) satisfies \(P(\theta\in C(D)\mid D)=0.95\) under this model. It differs from a frequentist 95% confidence interval, whose guarantee concerns coverage over repeated samples. Neither interval is guaranteed to be well-calibrated if its model is badly misspecified.

Bayes factors and prior odds

For hypotheses \(H_1,H_0\), posterior odds equal Bayes factor times prior odds:

\[ \frac{P(H_1\mid D)}{P(H_0\mid D)} =\underbrace{\frac{p(D\mid H_1)}{p(D\mid H_0)}}_{BF_{10}} \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_0)}. \]

For a composite hypothesis, \(p(D\mid H_j)=\int p(D\mid\theta_j,H_j)\pi_j(\theta_j)\,d\theta_j\): the marginal likelihood averages likelihood over a proper model-specific prior. A Bayes factor is therefore not simply a ratio of likelihoods evaluated at each model’s MLE, except in the point-hypothesis example above. Conventional verbal scales such as “anecdotal” or “strong” evidence are arbitrary aids, not scientific cutoffs; the value and prior assumptions should be reported directly.

The source’s mnemonic scale labels \(BF_{10}=1\) neutral, roughly \(1\)–\(3\) weak or anecdotal support for \(H_1\), \(10\)–\(30\) strong support, and values above \(100\) extreme support. Reciprocals express support for \(H_0\): about \(1/3\)–\(1\) weak support and below \(1/100\) extreme support. Intermediate ranges are not magical gaps or universal thresholds. Posterior odds also depend on prior odds, so even a large \(BF_{10}\) need not make \(H_1\) probable when its prior probability is very low.

Positive predictive value of a significant result

Consider many prespecified tests where an effect is truly present with base-rate probability \(\pi\), power is \(1-\beta\), and the false-positive rate for no effect is \(\alpha\). Bayes’ rule gives the proportion of significant findings arising from true effects:

\[ PPV=P(\text{effect true}\mid\text{significant}) =\frac{(1-\beta)\pi}{(1-\beta)\pi+\alpha(1-\pi)}. \]

Writing prior odds \(R=\pi/(1-\pi)\) yields the cheat sheet’s formula \(PPV=(1-\beta)R/[(1-\beta)R+\alpha]\). If \(\pi=0.01\), power \(=0.80\), and \(\alpha=0.05\), then \(PPV=0.008/(0.008+0.0495)\approx0.139\). Even a nominally significant result may have low predictive value when the base rate is low. Sensitivity or power is \(TP/(TP+FN)\); specificity is \(TN/(TN+FP)=1-\alpha\) in this stylized binary setup; PPV or precision is \(TP/(TP+FP)\); accuracy is \((TP+TN)/(TP+FP+FN+TN)\).

This is a model for a collection of tests, not a generally valid posterior probability for one particular study. Publication selection, multiple testing, heterogeneous effects, and ambiguous definitions of “true effect” alter its inputs. A p-value alone does not specify \(\pi\) and cannot determine \(PPV\).

Practice and pitfalls

  • Choose priors from substantive knowledge and check sensitivity. “More data” tends to reduce prior influence under identifiable, well-specified models, but neither framework automatically becomes reliable at a particular sample size.
  • Keep \(P(D\mid H)\) distinct from \(P(H\mid D)\); Bayes’ rule needs a prior and evidence under competing possibilities to reverse the conditioning.
  • State whether a Bayes factor compares point or composite hypotheses, which priors define the composite models, and whether stopping or selection was part of the design.
  • Do not treat a conventional Bayes-factor label or a significant p-value as a decision rule without costs, prior plausibility, and effect magnitude.

Interview questions

What is the difference between likelihood and posterior? Likelihood scores parameter values by how well they predict the observed data but is not a probability distribution over parameters. Multiplying by a prior and normalizing produces the posterior.

How does a credible interval differ from a confidence interval? The credible interval carries model-conditional posterior probability for the parameter. A frequentist confidence procedure has long-run coverage for a fixed parameter under its assumptions.

Why can a positive significant finding be unreliable? If true effects are rare, false positives among many null tests can outnumber detected true effects. Power, type I error, base rates, and selection practices jointly determine predictive value.

Summary

Bayesian inference updates explicit prior assumptions with a sampling model. Bayes factors compare predictive evidence between hypotheses, and predictive-value calculations show why base rates matter when interpreting “positive” findings.
