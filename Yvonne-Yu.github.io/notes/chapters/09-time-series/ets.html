Exponential Smoothing and ETS

Learning goals

  • Calculate SES and Holt updates and explain exponential weighting.
  • Add damped trends and additive or multiplicative seasonality.
  • Read ETS notation and distinguish a smoothing rule from a probabilistic forecasting model.

Intuition

A forecast can maintain a small summary of the past: the current level \(\ell_t\), trend \(b_t\), and seasonal pattern \(s_t\). Each new observation updates these states. Recent data usually receive more weight, while older influence decays geometrically.

SES tracks a level; Holt adds a trend; Holt–Winters adds seasonality. ETS puts such forecasting methods in an error–trend–seasonality state-space framework, supporting likelihood estimation and prediction intervals.

Worked example

Seven observations, two models

Use GMV \((100,102,105,103,106,108,110)\), SES initial level 100, and \(\alpha=0.3\). Holt also starts at level 100 with trend 2 and trend smoothing \(\beta^*=0.2\).

Day Observation SES level Holt level Holt trend
1 100 100.000 101.400 1.880
2 102 100.600 102.896 1.803
3 105 101.920 104.789 1.821
4 103 102.244 105.527 1.605
5 106 103.371 106.792 1.537
6 108 104.760 108.230 1.517
7 110 106.332 109.823 1.532

For example, the second SES update is \(0.3(102)+0.7(100)=100.6\). Holt’s first update is \(\ell_1=0.3(100)+0.7(100+2)=101.4\), followed by \(b_1=0.2(101.4-100)+0.8(2)=1.88\).

Using unrounded intermediate states, SES forecasts 106.332 at every horizon. Holt forecasts 111.355 one day ahead, 112.887 two days ahead, and 120.548 seven days ahead. The corrected arithmetic avoids accumulating the source table’s intermediate rounding differences. Holt follows the rising series more directly, but seven points do not establish superior out-of-sample accuracy.

Damp the distant trend

With damping \(\phi=0.9\), the trend multiplier is \(\sum_{j=1}^h\phi^j\). It equals 0.9 at one step, about 3.686 at five, 5.862 at ten, and approaches 9 as \(h\) grows. Thus long-run trend contribution is bounded instead of increasing forever. A fitted damped model also damps the state updates; applying damping only to an undamped fit is merely a numerical illustration.

Reuse the correct seasonal state

For daily data with weekly period \(m=7\), let current time be \(t=10\). At horizon \(h=5\), use seasonal state \(s_8\), since day 15 and day 8 share a weekday. At \(h=10\), use \(s_6\), since day 20 and day 6 are two weeks apart. The rule \(k=\lfloor(h-1)/m\rfloor+1\) selects \(s_{t+h-mk}\) from the most recent observed cycle.

Theory and assumptions

Simple exponential smoothing

\[\ell_t=\alpha Y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1},\qquad\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=\ell_t.\]

Unrolling the recursion gives

\[\ell_t=\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^jY_{t-j}+(1-\alpha)^t\ell_0.\]

The observation weights \(\alpha,\alpha(1-\alpha),\alpha(1-\alpha)^2,\ldots\) explain “exponential.” Large \(\alpha\) reacts quickly but follows noise; small \(\alpha\) smooths heavily. The initial state’s remaining weight matters early in a short series.

SES has flat multi-step point forecasts because it has no trend state. This does not imply zero future uncertainty. Parameters and initial states can be estimated by a suitable loss or likelihood; Gaussian additive-error formulations connect likelihood and squared errors, whereas other error models need not have the same objective.

Holt and damped Holt

\[\begin{aligned} \ell_t&=\alpha Y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\ b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)b_{t-1},\\ \widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}&=\ell_t+hb_t. \end{aligned}\]

The level combines the observation with yesterday’s level-plus-trend prediction. The trend combines the latest level change with the previous trend. Damped Holt replaces the old trend contribution by \(\phi b_{t-1}\) in the state updates and uses

\[\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=\ell_t+\left(\sum_{j=1}^h\phi^j\right)b_t.\]

For \(0<\phi<1\), the sum is \(\phi(1-\phi^h)/(1-\phi)\) and converges to \(\phi/(1-\phi)\). At \(\phi=1\), ordinary Holt is recovered.

Additive Holt–Winters

With period \(m\) and component-form smoothing parameters,

\[\begin{aligned} \ell_t&=\alpha(Y_t-s_{t-m})+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\ b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)b_{t-1},\\ s_t&=\gamma(Y_t-\ell_{t-1}-b_{t-1})+(1-\gamma)s_{t-m},\\ \widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}&=\ell_t+hb_t+s_{t+h-mk}. \end{aligned}\]

The level removes the previous same-season effect from the observation. The seasonal update compares the observation with the nonseasonal prediction. The forecast adds level, extrapolated trend, and the corresponding historical seasonal state.

For example, if \(Y_t=125\), old level 100, old trend 2, old same-season effect 20, and \((\alpha,\beta^*,\gamma)=(0.3,0.2,0.1)\), then \(\ell_t=0.3(105)+0.7(102)=102.9\), \(b_t=0.2(2.9)+0.8(2)=2.18\), and \(s_t=0.1(23)+0.9(20)=20.3\).

Different texts parameterize the seasonal update using the new rather than prior level; parameters must be transformed appropriately before treating formulas as identical.

Multiplicative seasonality

For proportional seasonal variation, replace deseasonalizing subtraction by division:

\[\begin{aligned} \ell_t&=\alpha(Y_t/s_{t-m})+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\ s_t&=\gamma\frac{Y_t}{\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}}+(1-\gamma)s_{t-m},\\ \widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}&=(\ell_t+hb_t)s_{t+h-mk}. \end{aligned}\]

The trend update remains the corresponding Holt update. An additive season might add 20 units every weekend; a multiplicative season might multiply the level by 1.2. Multiplicative models require suitable positive values and nonzero denominators. A log transform can turn proportional structure into additive structure, but back-transforming a mean forecast requires attention to bias.

ETS notation and innovations

ETS stands for Error, Trend, Seasonal, not three interchangeable component positions. Errors are additive or multiplicative; trend can be none, additive, damped additive, and in broader taxonomies multiplicative variants; seasonality can be none, additive, or multiplicative.

Model Interpretation
ETS(A,N,N) SES with additive errors
ETS(A,A,N) Holt with additive errors
ETS(A,A_d,N) Damped additive trend, no seasonality
ETS(A,A,A) Additive trend and additive seasonality
ETS(M,A,M) Multiplicative errors and seasonality, additive trend

The conceptual \(2\times5\times3=30\) taxonomy is not a promise that every software function fits every combination. Some combinations are excluded for numerical or statistical reasons; a component-smoothing API is not necessarily an automatic ETS model-selection API.

For ETS(A,N,N), the innovations form is

\[Y_t=\ell_{t-1}+\epsilon_t,\qquad\ell_t=\ell_{t-1}+\alpha\epsilon_t.\]

Subtracting observations across time yields

\[\Delta Y_t=\epsilon_t+(\alpha-1)\epsilon_{t-1},\]

an ARIMA(0,1,1) representation with the plus-sign MA convention and no drift. This is a specific equivalence; nonlinear/multiplicative ETS models do not all have an equivalent linear ARIMA model.

Practice and pitfalls

Inspect level, trend, seasonal amplitude, zeros, missing dates, and special events. Compare additive and multiplicative candidates using temporal holdouts and appropriate likelihood/information criteria. Estimate initial seasonal states from sufficient cycles rather than inventing a weekly pattern from fewer than seven observations.

A weekly period in daily observations is 7; weekly observations with annual seasonality have a period near 52, not 7. Keep units explicit.

For e-commerce daily activity, weekly seasonality and promotion spikes may need separate treatment. Standard single-seasonal ETS does not automatically model annual and weekly cycles together or external promotion effects. Multi-seasonal approaches, regression-based forecasts, or other model families may be useful comparisons. VAR/VECM and probabilistic neural forecasting address different multivariate or scale needs and should not be selected solely because ETS underfits one pattern.

Use rolling-origin evaluation, compare with seasonal-naive forecasts, and inspect residual autocorrelation and interval coverage. MAPE is unsuitable around zeros; MAE, RMSE, and scaled errors offer alternatives. A sample’s illustrative 4% error is not a general performance claim.

Interview questions

Why exponential weighting? Repeated updates multiply each older observation’s weight by \(1-\alpha\), producing a geometric sequence.

Why does SES forecast a flat line? It estimates only a level. Trend and seasonality need additional states.

When additive versus multiplicative? Fixed absolute seasonal amplitude suggests additive; amplitude proportional to the level suggests multiplicative, subject to positivity and validation.

ETS versus ARIMA? ETS organizes evolving level/trend/seasonal states; ARIMA organizes dependence in observations and innovations. Some cases coincide, and realistic holdout forecasts should guide the choice.

Summary

Exponential smoothing updates compact states as data arrive. ETS adds an explicit error model; correct parameterization, seasonal indexing, and temporal validation turn the update equations into a defensible forecasting workflow.
