ARIMA and Seasonal ARIMA

Learning goals

  • Read AR, MA, integration, and seasonal lag notation without confusing innovations with rolling averages.
  • Calculate simple forecasts, assess stationarity, and interpret ACF/PACF patterns.
  • Design temporal validation and residual diagnostics that respect a forecasting information set.

Intuition

Tomorrow’s GMV may depend on recent GMV and on recent surprises. An AR term uses past observations; an MA term uses past innovations. If the level drifts, differencing can expose a more stable process in changes. Seasonal terms repeat this logic at lags such as seven days or twelve months.

ARIMA is a statistical dependence model, not a causal explanation. Yesterday’s predictive information does not by itself identify what an intervention would do tomorrow.

Worked example

From levels to changes

For seven days of GMV \((100,102,105,103,106,108,110)\), first differences are

\[(2,3,-2,3,2,2),\qquad \overline{\Delta Y}=10/6\approx1.667.\]

The differences no longer show the same rising level, but six values cannot establish stationarity. A random-walk-with-drift baseline would forecast \(110+1.667=111.667\) next day if this estimated drift were used. Its uncertainty should reflect limited estimation data.

AR and MA predictions

For an AR(1) with \(c=20\), \(\phi=0.8\), and latest value 110,

\[\widehat Y_{t+1\mid t}=20+0.8(110)=108.\]

The stationary mean is \(20/(1-0.8)=100\), so the forecast moves toward 100. A second step gives \(20+0.8(108)=106.4\).

For an MA(1) with \(c=105\), \(\theta=0.3\), and latest inferred shock \(\hat\epsilon_t=5\),

\[\widehat Y_{t+1\mid t}=105+0.3(5)=106.5.\]

The unknown future innovation has conditional mean zero. Two steps ahead, this finite MA(1) shock no longer enters the mean forecast, so the forecast is 105. MA here means a moving average of innovations, not \((Y_t+Y_{t-1})/2\).

Reconstruct the level after differencing

Suppose a fitted ARIMA(1,1,1) has

\[W_t=0.5+0.4W_{t-1}+\epsilon_t+0.2\epsilon_{t-1},\qquad W_t=\Delta Y_t.\]

With latest difference 2 and inferred shock 1, next difference is \(0.5+0.4(2)+0.2(1)=1.5\). The next level forecast is \(110+1.5=111.5\). The following difference is \(0.5+0.4(1.5)=1.1\), because the future innovation is forecast as zero. Thus the two-step level forecast is 112.6.

Theory and assumptions

Notation and stationarity

\(Y_t\) is the observed value, \(\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}\) an \(h\)-step forecast using information through \(t\), and \(\epsilon_t\) an innovation. Weak white noise has zero mean, constant finite variance, and zero serial covariance; independence is a stronger condition, not part of every white-noise definition. For the conditional-mean forecasts below, assume \(E[\epsilon_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]=0\); weak white noise alone only supports the corresponding linear-prediction argument.

Weak stationarity requires constant mean and variance, and covariance depending on lag rather than calendar time. A trend, changing seasonal amplitude, or structural break can violate it. Differencing addresses certain nonstationary mean structures; a log or variance-stabilizing transform addresses a different issue. Deterministic trend regression and stochastic differencing are not interchangeable assumptions.

ADF tests a unit-root null under its selected deterministic/lag specification. KPSS tests a level- or trend-stationarity null under its specification. Failure to reject is not proof of the null, and breaks or low power can complicate both tests. Use plots, scientific context, and diagnostics alongside them.

AR(p)

\[Y_t=c+\sum_{j=1}^p\phi_jY_{t-j}+\epsilon_t.\]

The roots of \(1-\phi_1z-\cdots-\phi_pz^p\) must lie outside the unit circle for the usual causal stationary AR representation. In AR(1), \(|\phi|<1\) means shocks decay; \(\phi=1\) gives a random walk, and \(|\phi|>1\) gives an explosive recursion.

For a stationary AR with mean \(\mu\), \(c=\mu(1-\sum_j\phi_j)\). A differenced process can still have a nonzero mean or drift; differencing does not automatically set its constant to zero.

MA(q)

\[Y_t=c+\epsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_j\epsilon_{t-j}.\]

A finite linear combination of weak white noise with constant coefficients is weakly stationary. Invertibility is a separate condition ensuring an appropriate unique innovation representation from past observations; with this plus-sign convention, roots of \(1+\theta_1z+\cdots+\theta_qz^q\) lie outside the unit circle.

Innovations are not directly observed. Fitting infers them jointly with model parameters, often via likelihood/state-space methods. Forecasting uses estimated past innovations and zero conditional means for future innovations.

ARIMA(p,d,q)

Let \(LY_t=Y_{t-1}\) be the lag operator and \(W_t=(1-L)^dY_t\). Model \(W_t\) as ARMA:

\[W_t=c+\sum_{j=1}^p\phi_jW_{t-j}+\epsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^q\theta_j\epsilon_{t-j}.\]

\(d=0\) models levels, \(d=1\) models changes, and \(d=2\) models changes in changes, \(Y_t-2Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}\). Use as much differencing as needed for the chosen process, not as much as possible. Over-differencing adds unnecessary noise and can create problematic moving-average behavior.

Seasonal ARIMA

Write SARIMA\((p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s\), where lowercase orders refer to ordinary lags and uppercase orders to seasonal lags. Typical integer periods are 7 for weekly seasonality in daily data, 12 for annual seasonality in monthly data, and 4 for quarterly annual seasonality.

\[\Phi(L^s)\phi(L)(1-L^s)^D(1-L)^dY_t =c+\Theta(L^s)\theta(L)\epsilon_t.\]

For example, \((1-L)(1-L^{12})Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}-Y_{t-12}+Y_{t-13}\). Multiplying seasonal and ordinary AR polynomials also creates cross-lags; SARIMA is more than adding two isolated regressors.

A single seasonal period does not automatically capture both weekly and annual cycles. Annual patterns in daily data, leap years, moving holidays, and multiple seasonalities may call for calendar regressors, Fourier terms, decomposition, or other models. A fractional “365.25” is not a literal integer SARIMA lag.

ACF and PACF

ACF at lag \(k\) is \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-k})/\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)\) for a stationary process. PACF removes the linear association mediated by intermediate lags; “direct” here is statistical rather than causal.

Population model ACF PACF
AR(p) Tails off, sometimes oscillates Zero after lag p
MA(q) Zero after lag q Tails off
ARMA(p,q) Usually tails off Usually tails off

Sample plots are noisy estimates. “Cutoff” does not mean every later observed spike must lie inside a 95% band. Inspect seasonal lags as well as ordinary ones. Use AIC/AICc/BIC to compare appropriate candidates, then compare forecasts on rolling-origin holdouts. Automated order search does not remove the need to audit differencing, seasonal period, and residuals.

Practice and pitfalls

Fit transformations, model selection, and parameters using only data available at each forecast origin. Compare with naive, seasonal-naive, and drift baselines at the actual deployment horizon. Use MAE/RMSE and an appropriate scaled metric; MAPE is unstable with zeros and tiny denominators.

Inspect residual time plots, ACF, and Ljung–Box tests for remaining serial correlation. Ljung–Box tests autocorrelation through selected lags; a large p-value does not prove independence. A Q–Q plot assesses distributional tails relevant to model-based intervals. Residual normality is not necessary for every useful point forecast.

Prediction intervals often widen with horizon, but stationary-process uncertainty can approach a finite limit. Reconstructing levels after differencing accumulates uncertainty differently. Distinguish prediction intervals from confidence intervals for a conditional mean.

For a major-sale GMV forecast, known campaign dates can enter as regressors, while weekly seasonality and annual patterns need suitable modeling. Future regressor values must actually be known or forecast. Do not transfer an example’s claimed MAPE to a new dataset. Structural breaks can defeat an otherwise well-fitted historical model.

ARIMA offers interpretable linear dependence and strong short-horizon baselines. ETS emphasizes level/trend/seasonal states; decomposable trend/holiday approaches such as Prophet offer another model family. VAR/VECM concern multivariate dependence with their own stationarity/cointegration assumptions. Choose by temporal validation and the forecasting problem, not by a universal ranking.

Interview questions

What is MA(1)? A model using the current innovation and one past innovation, not a rolling mean of observed outcomes.

How do you select p, d, and q? Establish an appropriate stationary representation, use ACF/PACF and information criteria for candidate orders, and validate at realistic rolling forecast origins.

Can ADF prove stationarity? It can reject a specified unit-root null. That is not a blanket proof against every form of nonstationarity.

What about multiple seasonalities and promotions? Represent calendar information explicitly or choose a suitable multi-seasonal/decomposition model, then evaluate with future information restricted to what deployment knows.

Summary

ARIMA models observations and innovations after an appropriate transformation. Forecast quality requires correct timing, seasonal structure, residual checks, and comparisons with simple baselines; order selection alone is insufficient.
