XGBoost: Deriving Leaf Weights and Split Gain

Learning goals

Derive gradient and Hessian statistics, regroup them by leaf, solve for optimal leaf weights, calculate split gain, and connect the mathematics to engineering controls.

Intuition

A gradient provides a direction; curvature describes how quickly that gradient changes. XGBoost’s tree objective uses a second-order approximation to calculate regularized leaf updates. It also charges for the number of leaves and their weight magnitudes.

Use \(f_m\) for the new tree and \(h_i\) for a scalar second derivative to avoid confusing a function with a Hessian entry. These updates are in score space; for binary logistic loss, probabilities follow after the sigmoid.

Worked example

Take \(x=(1,2,3)\), \(y=(1,0,1)\), current logits zero, \(\lambda=1\), leaf penalty \(\gamma=0\), and shrinkage \(\nu=0.1\). Then

\[g=(-0.5,0.5,-0.5),\qquad h=(0.25,0.25,0.25).\]

For the split \(x\le1.5\), \(G_L=-0.5,H_L=0.25,G_R=0,H_R=0.5\). Its gain is

\[\frac12\left[\frac{0.25}{1.25}+0-\frac{0.25}{1.75}\right]\approx0.02857.\]

The split at \(2.5\) ties by symmetry. Selecting \(1.5\) gives \(w_L=0.4,w_R=0\). After shrinkage, logits are \((0.04,0,0)\) and probabilities approximately \((0.5100,0.5,0.5)\). Gradients cancel in the right leaf; later rounds can separate its conflicting labels.

Theory and assumptions

1. State the objective

For a tree with \(T\) leaves and weights \(w_j\),

\[\mathcal O_m=\sum_iL(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)+f_m(x_i))+ \gamma T+\frac\lambda2\sum_jw_j^2.\]

\(\gamma\) charges for leaves and \(\lambda\) shrinks their outputs. Define

\[g_i=\partial_FL(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)),\qquad h_i=\partial_F^2L(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)).\]

Squared half-error gives \(g_i=F_i-y_i,h_i=1\). Binary log-loss gives \(g_i=p_i-y_i,h_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). A small Hessian is low local curvature; it does not imply low loss or a correct prediction. A confidently wrong prediction can have small Hessian and large gradient.

2. Taylor-expand, dropping constants

\[\widetilde{\mathcal O}_m=\sum_i\left[g_if_m(x_i)+\frac12h_if_m(x_i)^2\right] +\gamma T+\frac\lambda2\sum_jw_j^2.\]

For a general smooth loss this is a local approximation. Squared error makes the quadratic expansion exact. The usual closed-form argument needs \(H_j+\lambda>0\); arbitrary custom losses need care when curvature is nonpositive.

3. Regroup by leaves

Let \(I_j\) contain the samples routed to leaf \(j\). Since \(f_m(x_i)=w_j\) in that leaf, define \(G_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}g_i\) and \(H_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}h_i\). Then

\[\widetilde{\mathcal O}_m=\sum_j\left[G_jw_j+\frac12(H_j+\lambda)w_j^2\right]+\gamma T.\]

Each leaf is now an independent scalar quadratic conditional on the tree structure.

4. Differentiate and substitute

\[G_j+(H_j+\lambda)w_j=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda}.\]

Substitution gives

\[\widetilde{\mathcal O}_m^*=-\frac12\sum_j\frac{G_j^2}{H_j+\lambda}+\gamma T.\]

This is the optimized surrogate contribution, not the full original loss. It enables comparison of candidate structures without separately optimizing every weight numerically.

5. Compare parent and children

Gain means parent objective minus child objective:

\[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Gain}=\frac12\bigg[ &\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}\\ &-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda} \bigg]-\gamma. \end{aligned}\]

Split only when the gain and other structural constraints permit it. With positive L2 regularization, the bracket need not be nonnegative: \(G_L=G_R=H_L=H_R=\lambda=1\) gives \(1/2+1/2-4/3=-1/3\). The two child penalties alter the comparison. Do not apply the unregularized Cauchy–Schwarz argument to this expression unchanged.

With an additional L1 term \(\alpha|w_j|\), the leaf solution becomes \(-\operatorname{sign}(G_j)(|G_j|-\alpha)_+/(H_j+\lambda)\) under this objective convention.

Practice and pitfalls

Engineering ideas

Quantile or histogram candidates reduce the number of thresholds. Sparse-aware splitting learns a default direction by comparing missing values sent left versus right. Sorted column blocks and parallel scans improve throughput; cache-aware storage reduces memory stalls; external-memory approaches process data exceeding RAM. These are implementation mechanisms, distinct from the derivation of the objective. Histogram methods still accumulate changing gradient statistics during training.

Tuning controls

Parameter family Role
eta, number of rounds Shrinkage and total additive complexity
max_depth Interaction and path complexity
min_child_weight Minimum child Hessian mass, not generally row count
gamma Minimum gain / leaf-complexity threshold
lambda, alpha L2 and L1 leaf penalties
subsample, colsample_bytree Row and feature sampling

Start by constraining depth and child support; assess sampling and penalties; then tune rate and rounds together with validation-based stopping. Record objective and implementation settings before interpreting a parameter numerically.

XGBoost is a system implementing boosted models, whereas gradient boosting is a broader algorithmic framework. Avoid absolute comparisons claiming all other GBDT implementations lack regularization, missing-value handling, or second-order leaf updates. The official objective tutorial documents the regularized boosted-tree formulation.

Interview questions

Why sum gradients by leaf? All observations in a leaf receive the same update, so their quadratic contributions combine into sufficient statistics \(G_j,H_j\).

What happens when \(H_j\) is tiny? An unregularized Newton step can become large; L2, child support constraints, and shrinkage help control it.

Where does \(-\gamma\) in gain come from? Replacing one leaf by two adds one leaf penalty.

Summary

Remember the sequence: differentiate loss, form a quadratic, group by leaf, solve its weight, compare parent and children, and shrink the chosen update.
