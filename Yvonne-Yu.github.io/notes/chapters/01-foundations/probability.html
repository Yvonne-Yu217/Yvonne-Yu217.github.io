Probability, Random Variables, and Distribution Shape

Learning goals

After this chapter, you should be able to define a probability space, calculate a conditional probability, explain what a random variable actually is, and distinguish raw, central, and standardized moments. Only elementary algebra is assumed.

Intuition

Inference asks what a limited observation says about an uncertain process. Probability supplies the rules for that uncertainty. A deterministic implication such as “if it rained, the pavement is wet” may fail as a description of the world because other conditions affect wetness, such as evaporation or a roof. Logic remains valid given its premises; probability helps represent uncertain premises and observations.

Imagine a lock that displays one digit. The sample space is \(\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,9\}\). One observed digit \(\omega\) is an outcome. An event is a set of outcomes, such as \(A=\{0,2,4,6,8\}\). For this finite example, the event collection is the power set \(\mathcal F=2^\Omega\). More generally, \(\mathcal F\) is a sigma-algebra; it need not contain every subset of an infinite sample space. A probability measure \(P:\mathcal F\to[0,1]\) completes the probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\).

Worked example

Suppose the ten lock digits are equally likely. Let \(A\) mean “even” and \(B\) mean “at least five.” Then \(P(A)=5/10\), \(P(B)=5/10\), and \(A\cap B=\{6,8\}\) has probability \(2/10\). The union rule gives

\[ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)=0.5+0.5-0.2=0.8. \]

Once we learn that the digit is even, our relevant sample space is restricted to the five even digits. Two satisfy \(B\), so

\[ P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}=\frac{0.2}{0.5}=0.4. \]

The multiplication rule reverses this calculation: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\). Because \(0.4\ne P(B)=0.5\), these particular events are not independent. Independence would require \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).

Now define \(X(\omega)=1\) for an even digit and \(0\) otherwise. The mapping \(X:\Omega\to\mathbb R\) is fixed; uncertainty comes from which \(\omega\) occurs. Here \(X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)\), so \(E[X]=0.5\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.25\).

Theory and assumptions

Axioms and calculation rules

Kolmogorov’s axioms say \(P(E)\ge0\), \(P(\Omega)=1\), and \(P(\bigcup_i E_i)=\sum_iP(E_i)\) for pairwise disjoint events. Finite additivity follows. The subtraction in the union formula prevents double-counting the intersection. Conditional probability requires \(P(A)>0\); independence is a separate property, not a consequence of conditioning.

Moments describe a distribution

For a continuous \(X\) with density \(f\), the \(k\)th raw moment is \(E[X^k]=\int x^kf(x)\,dx\). The \(k\)th central moment is \(E[(X-\mu)^k]\); the standardized moment divides by \(\sigma^k\) when \(\sigma>0\). For discrete variables, replace the integral by a sum. The zeroth raw moment is \(1\), the first raw moment is the mean \(\mu\), and the second central moment is the variance \(\sigma^2\). The third and fourth central moments, after standardization, describe skewness and kurtosis. Kurtosis concerns tail weight together with the distribution’s center and shoulders, so it is not a simple standalone “tail probability.” A center-of-mass analogy makes the mean intuitive: observations farther from the balancing point have greater leverage on it.

Normal approximation, Edgeworth, and Cauchy

A normal distribution is fully specified by its mean and variance:

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]. \]

Under appropriate independence or weak-dependence and finite-variance conditions, sums of many contributions can be approximately normal. This does not imply every quantity assembled from many causes is normal. An Edgeworth expansion refines a normal approximation for a standardized statistic using higher cumulants, including skewness and kurtosis; it is an asymptotic correction whose accuracy depends on regularity conditions, not a universal replacement for the normal law.

The standard Cauchy distribution has \(f(x)=1/[\pi(1+x^2)]\). Its mean and variance do not exist: the positive and negative parts of the expectation integral diverge, even though a symmetric principal value can be written as zero. For independent standard Cauchy observations, the sample mean is again standard Cauchy. The usual finite-mean law of large numbers and finite-variance central limit theorem therefore do not apply. Its tails are far heavier than normal tails.

Practice and pitfalls

  • State the sampling mechanism before assigning equal probabilities to outcomes; a lock digit is uniform only if the mechanism makes it so.
  • Distinguish an event \(A\subseteq\Omega\) from a random variable \(X:\Omega\to\mathbb R\) and its observed value \(X(\omega)\).
  • Do not write \(E[(X-\mu)^1]=\mu\); the first central moment is zero. Do not call an unstandardized fourth moment “kurtosis.”
  • A finite sample always has a numerical mean, even when the population mean is undefined. Its instability is the issue.

Interview questions

What changes when you condition on an event? The denominator changes from all possible outcomes to outcomes in the conditioning event. I calculate \(P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A)\) only when \(P(A)>0\).

Is a random variable itself random? Formally it is a measurable function of an uncertain outcome. Once the outcome is fixed, its value is fixed.

Why might a normal approximation fail? Heavy tails, dependence, rare-event structure, or insufficient sample size can defeat the conditions or convergence rate. A Cauchy sample mean is a striking counterexample.

Summary

Probability begins with outcomes, events, and a measure. Conditional probabilities change the reference event, while random variables turn outcomes into numerical quantities. Moments summarize distributions when they exist; heavy-tailed distributions remind us that they need not.
