Descriptive Statistics, Association, and Visual Checks

Learning goals

You should be able to select a center and spread measure for a data type, calculate Pearson and rank correlation, describe Chatterjee’s directional dependence coefficient, and explain why plotting data precedes interpretation. Review the probability chapter’s mean and variance first.

Intuition

A summary is a deliberate compression. Squared-error loss favors the mean; absolute-error loss favors the median; a mode reports the most frequent category. No single number retains the shape of a dataset. The same caution applies to association: Pearson measures linear alignment, Spearman measures monotone rank alignment, and more general dependence measures ask whether knowing one variable helps predict another.

Worked example

For \(x=(1,2,2,3,12)\), the mean is \(4\), the median and mode are both \(2\), and the population-style variance of these five values is

\[ \frac{(1-4)^2+(2-4)^2+(2-4)^2+(3-4)^2+(12-4)^2}{5}=16.4. \]

The corresponding standard deviation is \(\sqrt{16.4}\approx4.05\). The mean absolute deviation around the mean is \((3+2+2+1+8)/5=3.2\). The median absolute deviation around the median, another statistic also abbreviated MAD, is \(\operatorname{median}(1,0,0,1,10)=1\). Naming the center and aggregation rule prevents ambiguity. The single value \(12\) pulls the mean and standard deviation much more than it moves the median.

For \(X=(1,2,3)\) and \(Y=(2,4,6)\), the sample Pearson correlation is \(1\): the centered coordinates are proportional. Changing \(Y\) from centimeters to inches changes covariance but leaves correlation \(1\).

Theory and assumptions

Center and dispersion as loss choices

The minimizers of \(\sum_i(x_i-\theta)^2\), \(\sum_i|x_i-\theta|\), and \(\sum_i\mathbf1(x_i\ne\theta)\) are respectively the arithmetic mean, any median, and a mode (for a discrete sample). The last is a zero-one mismatch count, often called an \(L_0\)-style criterion; it is not a norm. Squaring heavily weights distant observations. For a population, \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\). For a sample used to estimate population variance, the familiar unbiased formula uses \(s^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)\) rather than division by \(n\).

Covariance and three notions of association

Covariance \(E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]\) has units equal to the product of the variables’ units. Pearson correlation removes those units:

\[ r=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}}. \]

It lies in \([-1,1]\) and captures linear association; nonlinear dependence can coexist with \(r=0\). Spearman’s \(\rho\) is Pearson correlation on ranks. With no tied ranks, \(\rho=1-6\sum_i d_i^2/[n(n^2-1)]\), where \(d_i\) is the difference of paired ranks. With ties, compute Pearson on the appropriately averaged ranks instead of using that shortcut. Spearman detects monotone patterns such as \(y=x^3\) and is less affected by the magnitude of outliers, although ranks can still be changed by unusual observations.

Chatterjee’s \(\xi\) is directional: \(\xi(X,Y)\) asks about dependence of \(Y\) on \(X\) and need not equal \(\xi(Y,X)\). In the no-tie sample construction, sort pairs by \(X\), let \(r_i\) be the associated rank of \(Y\), then compute

\[ \xi_n(X,Y)=1-\frac{3\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|r_{i+1}-r_i|}{n^2-1}. \]

Its population value lies in \([0,1]\), equals zero under independence, and equals one when \(Y\) is almost surely a measurable function of \(X\) (under the usual formulation). The finite-sample statistic can be negative and is not \(1\) even for a perfectly ordered finite sample. For \(n=5\) and ranks \((1,2,3,4,5)\) it is \(1-3(4)/24=0.5\). Ties require the tied-data definition; a coefficient alone does not establish a useful predictive model or causality.

Shape and visual diagnosis

A normal distribution has light tails relative to a Cauchy distribution, which lacks a finite mean and variance. Anscombe’s quartet shows that datasets can have nearly identical means, variances, Pearson correlations, and fitted linear regressions while exhibiting a line, a curve, an outlier-driven association, or a leverage point. Plot the observations and residuals before trusting a small set of summaries.

Practice and pitfalls

  • Choose median and quantiles for highly skewed numerical data; keep mean and standard deviation when the estimand or downstream model explicitly concerns them.
  • Report sample size, units, missingness, and transformations with a correlation. A unit change leaves Pearson’s \(r\) unchanged under positive rescaling but changes covariance.
  • Correlation cannot settle direction of causation, common-cause confounding, or a spurious trend. A nonlinear curve can make both Pearson and Spearman near zero.
  • The no-ties Chatterjee formula and its finite-sample range differ from the idealized population interpretation; do not quote \([0,1]\) as an exact finite-sample guarantee.

Interview questions

Why is the median more robust to one extreme value? The median depends on order, so moving the largest value farther away usually leaves it unchanged. Squared deviations make the mean and standard deviation react strongly.

When would Pearson be near zero despite dependence? If \(X\) is symmetric around zero and \(Y=X^2\), positive and negative \(X\) values cancel in covariance, although \(Y\) is determined by \(X\).

Does \(r=1\) mean \(X\) causes \(Y\)? No. It means the observed points lie on a positive-slope line, assuming nonzero variance. A common cause or data construction may explain the line.

Summary

Summary statistics answer distinct questions and discard distinct information. Explain the chosen loss or relationship, then inspect the actual data geometry before claiming a pattern.
