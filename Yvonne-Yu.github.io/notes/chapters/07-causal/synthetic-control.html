Synthetic Control

Learning goals

  • Explain the donor pool, pre-treatment fit, convex weights, and post-treatment gap.
  • Calculate a synthetic outcome and understand why fit is necessary but insufficient.
  • Compare synthetic control with DiD and design meaningful placebo analyses.

Intuition

When one city launches a policy, no single untreated city may be a convincing comparator. A synthetic control combines several untreated cities so that their pre-policy outcomes and predictors resemble the treated city’s. The post-policy gap is interpreted through the assumption that this relationship would have continued without treatment.

One treated city does not mathematically rule out DiD. Synthetic control is attractive when a weighted donor combination is more defensible than any single comparison; inference with very few treated units remains challenging for either approach.

Worked example

Build a synthetic city

Suppose pre-policy optimization produces weights 0.42, 0.28, 0.20, and 0.10 on four donors. The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. A future untreated donor outcome vector is \((100,80,90,70)\) in consistent GMV units. Then

\[\widehat Y_t(0)=0.42(100)+0.28(80)+0.20(90)+0.10(70)=89.4.\]

If the treated city’s observed outcome is 98, the estimated time-\(t\) effect is \(98-89.4=8.6\), or about 9.62% relative to the synthetic level. This interpretation requires a credible untreated trajectory; the arithmetic alone does not identify a causal effect.

Check the fit

Suppose treated pre-period outcomes are \((70,75,80)\) and synthetic values are \((69,76,80)\). Pre-RMSPE is

\[\sqrt{[(70-69)^2+(75-76)^2+(80-80)^2]/3}=\sqrt{2/3}\approx0.816.\]

A post-period gap of 8.6 is large relative to this pre-fit error, but a coincident treated-city shock could still explain it. Reassigning “treatment” to donors and recomputing the entire procedure provides a distribution of placebo gaps. Compare post/pre error ratios and pre-fit quality, not only raw post gaps from donors of very different scale.

Theory and assumptions

The optimization

Let \(X_1\) contain treated-unit pre-treatment predictors and outcome summaries, and let columns of \(X_0\) contain donor equivalents. Traditional synthetic control chooses

\[\widehat w=\arg\min_w (X_1-X_0w)'V(X_1-X_0w),\qquad w_j\ge0,\quad\sum_jw_j=1.\]

\(V\) weights predictor discrepancies. Alternatively, a simple teaching version minimizes squared pre-period outcome gaps directly. All tuning must use pre-treatment information; including treated post outcomes leaks the answer into the counterfactual.

For post-treatment periods,

\[\widehat\tau_t=Y_{1t}-\sum_j\widehat w_jY_{jt}.\]

The convex restriction avoids unrestricted extrapolation but means the treated unit may lie outside the donor pool’s convex hull. Poor pre-fit is then informative: the donor pool may not support the comparison.

Why pre-fit can help

Under suitable outcome models, a long and well-matched pre-period can align observed predictors and latent factor exposure. This supports extrapolating the untreated relationship forward. Perfect fit is neither guaranteed by the optimizer nor proof that the post counterfactual is correct.

Important conditions include no anticipation, no donor contamination or spillovers, stable measurement, no treatment-related donor selection, and no unmodeled shock uniquely affecting the treated unit after launch. A stable factor structure or another substantive counterfactual argument is still required. Synthetic control does not remove causal assumptions by “constructing parallel trends.”

Placebos and uncertainty

An in-space placebo applies the same design to untreated donor units. An in-time placebo moves the intervention into the pre-period. Leave-one-donor-out analysis reveals dependence on a single heavily weighted donor. All are diagnostics; their inferential interpretation depends on comparability and an assignment or sampling argument.

With few donors, placebo ranks are coarse. A donor with terrible pre-fit is not comparable to a well-fitted treated unit without adjustment or restriction. A standard independent-observation t-test on serially correlated post gaps is usually inappropriate.

Practice and pitfalls

Situation Design implication
Many treated and control units with credible common trends DiD may offer a direct and transparent design
One or few treated units and a rich untreated donor panel Synthetic control can construct a more suitable comparator
Weak pre-fit Revise the donor pool/predictors or acknowledge insufficient support
Strong geography or seasonal differences Match pre-period trajectories and relevant predictors carefully
Donors exposed indirectly to the policy Exclude or explicitly model contamination

Normalize units and denominators, document donor exclusions, freeze the fitting period, and show weights alongside outcome plots. Compare absolute and relative gaps without confusing aggregate GMV effects with per-user effects.

The classic applications include the Basque Country study and California’s tobacco-control program. The methodological lesson is a transparent comparative case design; historical applications do not validate a new city’s counterfactual automatically.

Interview questions

Why not choose the most similar single city? A convex combination can match multiple pre-treatment features better while making the donor contribution explicit.

Can synthetic control fix failed parallel trends? It can construct a different comparator, but requires adequate pre-fit and a credible stable untreated relationship. It is not a guaranteed repair.

What do placebo gaps tell you? Whether similar apparent effects arise when the same procedure is applied without the intervention, conditional on comparable fit and the study design.

Summary

Synthetic control makes the counterfactual construction visible through donor weights and pre-fit. Its credibility rests on a stable untreated relationship, uncontaminated donors, and transparent sensitivity analyses.
