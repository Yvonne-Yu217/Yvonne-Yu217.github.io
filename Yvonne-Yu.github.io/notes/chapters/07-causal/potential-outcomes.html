Potential Outcomes and Causal Identification

Learning goals

  • Distinguish an individual effect, ATE, ATT, ATU, CATE, and assignment effects.
  • Explain consistency, interference, exchangeability, and overlap in business language.
  • Derive the adjustment formula and recognize when observed comparisons do not identify it.

Intuition

A causal question compares two interventions on the same population. If a customer receives a coupon, we observe spending with the coupon but cannot simultaneously observe spending without it. Predicting who spends most does not answer who spends more because of the coupon.

For person \(i\), define potential outcomes \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\), assignment \(T_i\in\{0,1\}\), and pre-treatment covariates \(X_i\). Consistency gives

\[Y_i=T_iY_i(1)+(1-T_i)Y_i(0).\]

The individual treatment effect is \(Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\). One outcome is missing for each person: the fundamental problem of causal inference. A model’s CATE prediction is an average among people with similar recorded characteristics; it does not reveal both outcomes for a particular person.

Worked example

The coupon counterfactual

In a hypothetical pair of worlds, a customer would spend 100 with a coupon and 60 without it, so her effect is 40. If she receives the coupon we see 100, not 60. A nonrecipient spending 50 is not automatically a valid replacement for her missing 60: targeting may favor people who already intend to buy.

Suppose 40% of a population receives treatment, and the true subgroup effects are ATT = 8 and ATU = 3. Then

\[\operatorname{ATE}=0.4(8)+0.6(3)=5.\]

Estimand Definition Business question
ATE \(E[Y(1)-Y(0)]\) What would treating everyone change on average?
ATT \(E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T=1]\) What did the intervention change for recipients?
ATU \(E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T=0]\) What might expansion change for current nonrecipients?
CATE \(\tau(x)=E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]\) Which observable groups benefit?

A high-frequency customer’s CATE might be 12 while a low-frequency customer’s is 1. These are hypothetical group averages, not measured individual effects. The identity ATE = \(P(T=1)\)ATT + \(P(T=0)\)ATU follows directly from total expectation.

Assignment versus behavior

A ranking experiment randomly assigns users to a new strategy. Its direct effect is the intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of assignment. Whether users actually click the resulting card is a later, self-selected behavior. Comparing clickers with nonclickers discards randomization.

A worked case in the source notes adds a score proportional to predicted click-through rate times predicted active return to a ranking policy. The transferable design lesson is to analyze the assigned policy at the randomization unit, with predeclared activity/order outcomes and platform guardrails. Those observations cannot identify the causal effect of clicking merely because the ranking assignment was randomized.

Theory and assumptions

Consistency and SUTVA

Consistency connects the observed intervention to the specified potential outcome. The treatment must have sufficiently well-defined versions: “saw an advertisement” may hide one-second and thirty-second exposures with different effects.

SUTVA also rules out interference between units in its usual formulation. Coupons shared with friends, competition for limited inventory, driver supply changes, and recommendation-market equilibrium effects can affect untreated users. Cluster randomization or an explicit spillover model may address such questions, but changes the design and potentially the estimand.

Warning

Random assignment does not automatically guarantee SUTVA. It establishes a property of the assignment mechanism; interference and treatment versions still require design arguments.

Conditional exchangeability

\[\{Y(0),Y(1)\}\perp T\mid X.\]

Conditional on measured \(X\), treatment assignment provides no further information about either potential outcome. This concerns who gets treatment, not whether treatment works. A randomized drug can reduce pain even though assignment is independent of both potential pain scores.

Gym attendance and longevity illustrate failure: attendees may have better baseline health and higher income. Controlling recorded age, income, and health helps only if the remaining differences relevant to both attendance and longevity are addressed. Unrecorded motivation may still confound the comparison. Similarly, adjusting industry does not remove unmeasured diligence from an observational comparison of software adoption and productivity.

Exchangeability cannot generally be tested from observed outcomes because the missing potential outcomes remain missing. A causal diagram, timing audit, domain knowledge, negative controls, and sensitivity analysis support the argument; none proves it.

Positivity and overlap

\[0<e(x):=P(T=1\mid X=x)<1\]

is needed on the covariate support of the target population. A deterministic rule assigning all large sellers to support provides no untreated comparison for those sellers. Near-zero probabilities produce weak empirical information and unstable weights even when formal positivity holds.

Estimated probabilities close to zero or one can reflect overfitting, leakage, or genuine assignment rules. Plot both groups’ estimated score distributions and inspect relevant covariate strata. Restriction to common support changes the population being estimated. Another estimator cannot manufacture missing comparisons.

Identification step by step

For treatment \(t\) and supported \(x\),

\[\begin{aligned} E[Y\mid T=t,X=x] &=E[Y(t)\mid T=t,X=x] &&\text{consistency}\\ &=E[Y(t)\mid X=x] &&\text{exchangeability}. \end{aligned}\]

Overlap makes both treatment-arm conditional regressions estimable on the target support. Subtracting identifies CATE, and averaging over the target covariate distribution identifies ATE:

\[\operatorname{ATE}=E_X\{E[Y\mid T=1,X]-E[Y\mid T=0,X]\}.\]

Identification is a population statement. Estimation adds finite data, modeling error, and uncertainty; a causal assumption does not guarantee a good finite-sample estimate.

Practice and pitfalls

  1. Specify eligibility, intervention versions, outcome window, and target population.
  2. Draw a timeline and retain covariates measured before assignment or exposure.
  3. Separate the assignment effect from compliance or engagement effects.
  4. Audit spillovers, post-treatment selection, attrition, and missing outcomes.
  5. Defend the identifying assumptions before fitting a flexible model.
  6. Report effect size and uncertainty in absolute units and, where useful, relative units.

Do not claim that DiD or IV “fixes” an overlap problem without evaluating their different assumptions. Do not remove a real confounder simply to make treated and control score histograms look alike. Randomization balances baseline variables in expectation; realized imbalance and clustering still matter for precision and inference.

Interview questions

Does ignorability mean treatment has no effect? No. It states that assignment is independent of potential outcomes conditional on covariates. The contrast between the two potential outcomes may still be large.

Why is CATE not an individual treatment effect? CATE averages over all unmeasured variation among units with the same recorded \(X\). Individual counterfactuals remain unobserved.

What does an A/B test identify when users can ignore the feature? The assignment or ITT effect. A treatment-on-the-treated interpretation needs additional compliance assumptions and an appropriate design, such as a valid encouragement instrument.

Can I verify no unmeasured confounding? Generally not. I explain the assignment mechanism, audit covariate timing, and report sensitivity to plausible omitted factors.

Summary

A causal analysis starts with the intervention and estimand. Consistency, exchangeability, and overlap permit replacing missing potential outcomes with comparisons across observed people; they do not eliminate estimation error or resolve interference automatically.
