Instrumental Variables, 2SLS, and LATE

Learning goals

  • State relevance, independence, exclusion, and monotonicity separately.
  • Calculate the first stage, reduced form, Wald ratio, and two-stage estimate.
  • Interpret LATE for compliers and diagnose weak-instrument inference.

Intuition

Viewing a livestream and ordering may share an unobserved cause: shopping intent. A valid instrument \(Z\) changes viewing while providing variation unrelated to that intent and affecting ordering only through viewing. The estimate uses the treatment variation induced by \(Z\).

A randomized encouragement is a candidate instrument, not automatically a valid one. A card can alter brand awareness, displace other content, or change shopping intent even without a view; those are possible exclusion violations.

Worked example

Encouragement and the Wald ratio

Consider an explicitly hypothetical study of 1,000 users:

Assignment Users Livestream viewing rate Ordering rate
Encouragement \(Z=1\) 500 60% 12%
No encouragement \(Z=0\) 500 20% 6%

The first-stage effect is \(0.60-0.20=0.40\), and the reduced-form outcome effect is \(0.12-0.06=0.06\). Therefore

\[\widehat\beta_{Wald}=\frac{0.06}{0.40}=0.15.\]

The encouragement changes ordering by 6 percentage points. Under valid-IV and monotonicity assumptions, viewing changes ordering by 15 percentage points among compliers. Neither is a universal individual effect. The encouragement’s own policy value is directly represented by its reduced form, potentially adjusted for deployment costs.

See both regression stages

Stage one gives \(\widehat T=0.20+0.40Z\). The fitted treatment values are 0.20 and 0.60. Stage two fits an outcome line through \((0.20,0.06)\) and \((0.60,0.12)\), whose slope is 0.15. This reproduces the Wald estimate.

A direct observational regression of outcome on actual viewing may have a larger coefficient because it includes selection on shopping interest. Its magnitude cannot be decomposed into “true effect plus bias” numerically without additional information.

Theory and assumptions

Four distinct requirements

  1. Relevance: \(Z\) changes treatment probability, conditionally on included baseline controls if applicable.
  2. Independence: \(Z\) is independent of relevant potential outcomes and treatment responses, or satisfies the appropriate conditional exogeneity argument.
  3. Exclusion: holding treatment fixed, changing \(Z\) does not change the outcome. In potential-outcome notation, \(Y(t,z)=Y(t)\).
  4. Monotonicity for binary LATE: \(T_i(1)\ge T_i(0)\); the encouragement does not reduce treatment for any unit.

Consistency and a suitable interference assumption are also needed. Exclusion and independence are conceptually different; writing one covariance restriction does not establish both. Monotonicity can fail if an intrusive recommendation deters some users, so it must be argued rather than assumed to hold in all industrial settings.

Compliance types

Type \(T(0)\) \(T(1)\) Contribution to the first-stage change
Never-taker 0 0 None
Always-taker 1 1 None
Complier 0 1 Positive
Defier 1 0 Negative; excluded by monotonicity

Under monotonicity, the first-stage difference equals the complier fraction. Independence and exclusion make the outcome difference equal to that fraction times the complier average effect:

\[\begin{aligned} E[T\mid Z=1]-E[T\mid Z=0]&=P(C),\\ E[Y\mid Z=1]-E[Y\mid Z=0]&=P(C)E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid C]. \end{aligned}\]

Dividing identifies LATE. In the example \(P(C)=0.4\). The effects for always-takers and never-takers are not identified by this contrast. Scaling to a different encouragement or a new population may change the complier group.

Two-stage least squares

With baseline controls \(X\), first regress \(T\) on an intercept, \(Z\), and \(X\), then regress \(Y\) on fitted \(T\), an intercept, and the same controls. More generally, 2SLS projects the endogenous regressors onto the instrument space.

Use an IV estimator’s variance calculation. Running two ordinary regressions and accepting the second regression’s naive standard errors ignores the generated-regressor structure and uses the wrong residuals for structural inference. Cluster or heteroskedasticity-robust inference may be needed.

Weak instruments

The Wald ratio divides by the first stage. If that denominator is close to zero, small sampling changes can produce very large estimate changes, nonnormal distributions, and unreliable conventional confidence intervals. Report first-stage coefficients and appropriate strength statistics.

The familiar first-stage \(F>10\) is a rough historical diagnostic, not a universal guarantee of strong identification, especially with heteroskedasticity, clustering, many instruments, or multiple endogenous variables. Stock–Yogo critical values have their own assumptions; use diagnostics appropriate to the design.

Potential responses are a more informative valid instrument, a better-designed encouragement, carefully justified LIML in suitable settings, or weak-IV-robust procedures such as Anderson–Rubin inference. A wide or unbounded interval is information about the design’s limits. Adding controls does not guarantee a stronger or more valid instrument.

Practice and pitfalls

Historical candidate instruments illustrate the argument and its vulnerabilities:

Candidate Intended treatment variation Main question to defend
Birth quarter and schooling Compulsory-schooling rules Could season of birth relate to wages through other channels?
College proximity Cost/access to education Could geography also affect labor markets or family background?
Draft lottery Military service Is exclusion plausible for the specific outcome and design?
Randomized recommendation Actual engagement Does the recommendation affect outcome without engagement?
Rainfall and rides Ride usage Weather directly changes travel demand: exclusion is doubtful

The source notes include these as motivating examples; none should be described as valid merely because the story sounds external. In particular, weather is often a poor instrument for downstream travel outcomes because it directly changes them.

Report the treatment units, first stage, reduced form, LATE, confidence interval, compliance interpretation, and threats to exclusion. Distinguish “effect of offering the program” from “effect of participation for induced participants.”

Interview questions

What makes an instrument valid? It must shift treatment, be exogenous under the design, and have no outcome pathway except treatment. Binary LATE also needs monotonicity and a nonzero complier fraction.

Why LATE rather than ATE? Only compliers change treatment when the instrument changes. Their outcome response identifies the ratio; always-taker and never-taker treatment effects remain outside it.

Is a first-stage F of 11 enough? It is not a universal certificate. I examine the relevant weak-identification diagnostics, covariance structure, number of instruments, and robust inference.

Would a random card with no purchase button guarantee exclusion? No. It might still affect awareness or displace content. The absence of a button is evidence about one pathway, not proof about all pathways.

Summary

IV trades conditional exchangeability of treatment for a credible external variation argument. The output often targets compliers, and weak first stages or exclusion violations cannot be repaired by confident interpretation.
